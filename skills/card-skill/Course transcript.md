The words of the card course.

## Chapter 1. Why you forget, and the fix

Say you learn something today. A week from now, your brain will have forgotten most of it.

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus measured this on himself. One day after he learned a list, most of that work was gone. Learning it again took two thirds of the time.

In 2015, scientists repeated his experiment. A hundred and thirty years later, they got the same curve.

But review it again before you forget, and it sticks longer.

Each time, the gap grows. A day. A week. A month.

A new memory starts out fragile. Over time, the brain makes it stable. Scientists call this consolidation.

Each review, near the point where you would forget, pushes the memory further into long-term memory.

This is hard science. Researchers have watched it in brain cells: spaced practice switches on the genes that build long-term memory.

The work that first showed how brain cells store a memory won a Nobel Prize.

Scientists have tested this for more than a century. One review alone covered 317 experiments, and spacing won.

And yes, there is a best moment to review. A study of more than 1,300 people found it: too early or too late, and you keep less.

So how do you store each thing you learn in the app? Answer: on a card.

A card is one idea, with part of it hidden.

You will have many cards, one for each thing you want to keep.

The app runs an algorithm behind the scenes to keep track of this for you, and predicts the day you are about to forget it. This is called a spaced repetition algorithm.

That algorithm is called FSRS. It is a mathematical model of your memory.

For every card, every day, it works out the odds that you still remember it. When they drop to about ninety percent, the card comes back.

The algorithm finds that moment for every card, precisely for you, by studying your memory.

The app keeps a calendar for every card. On the day a card is due, it shows it to you.

You try to recall the hidden part, then check. And you answer: forgot, or remembered.

This course takes about half an hour, in twelve short chapters.

Some chapters let you try the steps in the real app. That part is always your choice.

Your brain forgets on a curve. Now you have a way to beat it. Next: what a card really is, and you make your first one.

## Chapter 2. The card: the hub of an idea

Quick recap: each thing you learn goes on a card. Now, what a card really is, and how to make one.

When most people hear "card", they picture a line or two on a small piece of paper.

Think of it another way. A card is the hub of one idea: the centre where what you know about it comes together.

Here is a real one. The front is the short version: the idea in a sentence or two, with the key part hidden.

The back has the details: how it works, an example, a hook to remember it by.

And at the bottom, links to the cards it connects to. You will make those in chapter 10.

Here is another, for a new word. The front has a sentence that uses the word, then what it means. The word is hidden in both.

Its back has the details too: how to say it, where it comes from, more examples.

So a card is more than one line, and far less than a page. The short version on the front, the details on the back.

Piotr Wozniak, who built SuperMemo, made it a rule: keep the front simple. A simple front is easier to remember.

A card is never finished. It starts light, and it grows as you learn more.

All your cards together are your card base. Each card is one idea, so cards can be mixed and matched, wherever you need them.

Your turn. Think of something you learned this week.

Type the short version of it, in a sentence or two.

Now tap the word you want to hide.

That's the front. The hidden word is the part you will try to recall.

Now the back. Add one detail: how it works, or an example.

Tap the card to check yourself.

Did you remember it?

Either answer is fine. The app saves it with the card.

Your card is now a real file, in a folder called Course Cards, at the top of your vault.

In the app, right-click a folder and choose New card. Type the title, press Enter, then the front.

To hide a word, select it, and click Hide word in the toolbar. The key is Command L.

For the back, type slash, and choose New side. The key is Command Shift Enter.

You can add sections to the back. Type three stars on an empty line, and they turn into a line. Sections break up the details, so they are easier to take in.

Press Escape, or click Done, and the card saves.

The title sits at the top of the card's tab. Click it to change it, and the file's name follows.

If the title gives the answer away, set Title on the front to Hide, in the card's menu.

To make one in the real app now, choose Try it in the app.

## Chapter 3. The Inbox, each day

You made your first card. Here is the daily habit that brings it back.

So which card do you look at, and when?

The app keeps track of the dates for you. All you do is open the Inbox every day.

The Inbox is in the sidebar. The number beside it is how many cards are due now.

The Inbox shows one card at a time. Look at the card, and try to recall what is hidden.

Trying to recall is what makes it stick. A study in the journal Science put it to the test.

People who kept testing themselves remembered 80 percent a week later. People who kept re-reading, with no testing, remembered 36 percent.

About the same amount of work. More than double the memory.

Press Space to show the hidden words, one at a time, and then the back.

Then choose Forgot or Remembered. Be honest. It only helps you.

Hold the pointer on a button to see when the card comes back.

You can also press 1 for Forgot and 2 for Remembered. Command Z takes back the last answer.

Based on that, the app moves the calendar for you.

If you forgot, the card comes back sooner, so you can learn it again.

In fact, a card you forgot comes back later in the same review, until you remember it.

If you remembered, the card waits longer. Each time you remember it, the wait grows.

A new card needs two Remembered in a row. Then it leaves for a day or more.

You never set a date. The app works out each date from your answers.

Missed a day? That's fine. The cards that are due wait in the Inbox until you come back.

To keep it light, the Inbox brings at most ten new cards a day. You can change that number in Settings.

When nothing is left, the Inbox says Inbox complete. That's it for today.

## Chapter 5. Why it is worth it

You can make, review and place a card. Now, why it is worth doing.

What you learn this way compounds. To compound is to grow on itself, like interest.

Each thing you keep gives the next thing something to hold on to.

Say you make five cards a day. In a year, that is more than 1,800 small things you still know.

And known cards come back less often, so the daily review stays small as the pile grows.

Follow one card you keep remembering. It comes back after a few days. Then about two weeks. Then about six weeks. Then about four months.

So a couple of months in, that card is already months away.

By then you are not really reviewing it any more. It is part of what you know. It is part of who you are.

The sociologist Niklas Luhmann kept a box of paper slips for about 45 years. It grew to around 90,000 of them.

He wrote nearly 600 publications, among them more than 40 books.

The writer Ryan Holiday has kept tens of thousands of index cards over twenty years. He says the most value came later, from all of them added up.

You do not need to write books. Think of the meeting where the right fact is there when you need it.

The language you keep. The subject you finally understand.

Over time, your card base becomes a record of what you know. Because you review it, you still know it.

Small cards, every day, add up. But only if they are truly yours: that is next.

## Chapter 6. Your card base is your knowledge base

Your cards add up, and what you know compounds. So what should go into them?

Your card base is your knowledge base. It is a picture of what you know.

The goal is not to copy the internet into it. There is always more.

The goal is to distil. Take what you learned, and put it in your own words and your own formats.

Think of it like digestion. You do not have to know a thing fully before you make its card.

But you do have to chew it first.

To chew is to think about it a little: read it, talk it over, put the idea in your own words.

The full digestion comes later. Each time you review the card, you digest it a little more.

Memory researchers call this depth of processing. It is one of the most cited ideas in the science of memory: the more you think about what something means, the longer you keep it.

That is why copying someone else's cards makes no sense: it skips the thinking that makes you learn.

So when you hear something worth keeping, write it down somewhere.

Do not make the card in that moment. Rushed cards are poor cards, they pile up, and review becomes a chore.

Make the card later, when you have time to sit and think. Capture first, create later.

With ten new cards a day and sixty waiting, a new card can take about a week to reach you.

When it comes, it is no surprise. You made it, you chewed on it, and you have been looking forward to it.

You carry a picture of your card base in your head, because you put each card there.

Your card base is your knowledge base: make it yours. Next: how to make a card you will be glad to see again.

## Chapter 7. What makes a good card

You have chewed on an idea. Here is how to make its card a good one.

Many people paste a whole page of text into one card. Then they review it, and nothing sticks.

A wall of text is not a card. It asks you to recall everything at once, so you recall nothing well.

A single bare line is the other extreme. You may recall the words, and still not understand the idea.

So before you make a card, ask one question. What is the short version of this idea?

That goes on the front, with the key part hidden. The details go on the back, in sections.

So a review is quick: you fill in the front. Then the back gives you the details again.

Now, how cards grow. Start with an umbrella: one card that covers a whole topic.

Say, how protein, carbs and fat differ. One light card is enough to start.

As you learn more about protein, it outgrows that card. So protein gets a card of its own.

The two link to each other. The umbrella stays the overview, and protein becomes a branch.

Over time you get a tree: the umbrella, its branches, and smaller branches from those. Each card is a node, a point where branches meet.

The tree grows as your understanding grows. So start light, and do not feel like you have to build the whole tree on day one.

Now, the front itself. Most people think a card is a question on one side, and the answer on the other.

You can ask a question. Or you can just write the idea as a sentence, and hide the key part.

Memory scientists call that cloze deletion. In this app, we just call them hidden words.

"DNS stands for", then a blank. You jump straight in and fill it. It is faster to make, and faster to review.

An example on the front helps too. With your agent, one is easy to get.

Your turn. Which of these two cards is better?

The second one. The first card hides everything that matters, so there is no clue to recall from.

So, rule one. Hide the answer, not the clue. The front must still make sense with the blank in it.

Reading is not recalling. Researchers graded ten of the most common ways to learn. Re-reading and highlighting got the lowest grade.

Only two got the top grade: testing yourself, and spacing it out over time. A card does both.

Rule two. Leave enough in view to point to one answer. A blank that could be many things teaches you nothing.

Rule three. Hide a phrase that means something, not a few scattered words.

When you check yourself, the same meaning in other words counts. Exact words matter only for quotes and commands.

Name each card after the idea, not after where you found it.

Different things need different formats: a word, a person, a process, a quote. Each has a short page in the card skill.

The short version on the front, the details on the back, and a tree that grows with you. Next, a tool that can help, and hurt.

## Chapter 8. Make cards with your AI, without vibe writing

A good card starts with chewing. Now, a tool that makes cards fast, and why that is a danger.

One fact matters here: each card is a plain Markdown file, plain text with a few light marks.

An agent is an AI tool that can read and write files on your computer. Any agent like that works here.

The app has no AI inside, and it sends nothing anywhere. You use your own agent.

Now the warning. Do not vibe write your cards.

Vibe writing means letting an AI pour out cards you never thought through.

It is the fastest way to ruin your card base, which is your knowledge base, and to make the whole system worthless.

If you want this system to fail, vibe write your cards. If you want what you know to compound, do not.

Ryan Holiday puts it this way: "The inefficiency is the efficiency." The thinking is where you learn.

The right way: bring ideas you have chewed on. Your agent drafts the cards in the chat, and you read each one there.

Let's try it. Paste something you have chewed on, or use the example.

Copy the prompt. It tells your agent to read the card skill, then draft three cards in the chat.

Paste it into your agent. Read each draft in the chat. Ask for a change, or say yes.

Only the cards you approve are saved. They show up here as your agent saves them.

If nothing shows up after a minute, check with your agent.

That is the handmade part. Reading the drafts, thinking about them, saying yes: that is chewing.

No agent? Skip this step. The course shows three example cards, and makes them only if you say so.

Your agent drafts fast, but you decide. Keep only what you want to recall, in your own words.

## Chapter 9. Four steps: capture, create, review, improve

Let's step back and see the whole system.

It has four steps: capture, create, review, and improve.

Capture happens all day, wherever you are. When something is worth keeping, write it down.

A note on your phone, a brain dump, a chat channel just for you. It does not matter where, as long as you have one place.

Create is when you sit down with your capture list, and turn it into cards.

This is where you chew. Your agent can do most of the work, and you read and approve each card.

Create is the one you set time aside for. Chewing takes thought, so give it a quiet moment.

Review is the daily one, here in the app. You open the Inbox, and the app picks the cards.

Trust the schedule, even when it feels slow. Your gut is a poor judge of your own memory.

In one experiment, spacing won for nine people out of ten. Yet seven out of ten walked away sure it had lost.

In the Science study, everyone guessed they would remember about half. Some kept 80 percent. Some kept a third. Nobody saw it coming.

Improving needs no time of its own. It happens while you review.

This card is not clear. That one needs a link. With an agent, you send the fix right then, and keep going.

Even with a large card base, improving should only take a couple of minutes a day.

Capture all day. Create with time set aside. Review every day. Improve as you review. Next: the idea that changes how you organise everything.

## Chapter 10. Links: how cards connect

Quick recap: you capture, create, review and improve. Now, how your cards connect, and why that beats folders.

Most of us keep things in files and folders. Computers taught us: one thing, one place.

So when you make a card, you ask: where does this go?

That is why the app does not require folders. It leaves the choice to you.

But with great power comes great responsibility. Our advice: keep folders few, and rely on links.

Make a folder only when you truly need one.

Links hold them together. You have seen one: the protein card, linked to its umbrella.

Your mind does not work like folders. It works by association: one idea calls up another.

Researchers describe memory as a web of linked ideas. Think of one, and the ones linked to it come to mind more easily.

And researchers found that new facts are usually remembered better when they fit with something you already know.

A tree has branches. But ideas also connect across trees, and a folder cannot show that.

An easy example. Say you did sort your cards into folders. Your protein card would sit in Food.

And your card on how muscle grows would sit in Exercise.

They belong together: your body builds muscle out of protein.

A card can live in only one folder, so the folders keep them apart. A link joins them.

Folders ask: where does this go? Links ask: what does this connect to?

A card can link to any card, wherever it lives, and to any page.

The easy way: ask your agent to find the connections.

Keep it handmade: the agent shows you each one in the chat, and you read it and approve it.

Beside each link go a few words on why the two connect. The reason is worth as much as the link.

To link by hand, edit the card. Type two square brackets, then a few letters of the other card's title.

A list shows what matches. Click the one with the card icon.

The link turns into a pill: the other card's title, with its ID small beside it.

Click the pill, and that card opens. Under it, Linked mentions lists what links to it.

Some cards end up with many links pointing to them. Those are your hubs: the ideas the rest leans on.

To remove a link, edit the card, and delete the pill like a word.

Your card base is your knowledge base. Links make it a base, not a pile.

To link two of your Course Cards, choose Try it in the app.

Ask what a card connects to, not where it goes. Next: how to keep your tree alive.

## Chapter 11. Be a gardener

Your cards form trees, joined by links. Now, how to improve them as you review.

Think of a gardener. A garden does well when someone clears it and prunes it.

That is not a chore. Each fix makes you think about the idea again, and that is learning too.

A card you keep missing is a sign to look at it. Often it is unclear, or it asks too much at once.

To fix a card, tell your agent what to change, and keep reviewing. Each card is a file, so it can.

Or do it yourself: click Edit card, or press E in the Inbox. Press Escape when you are done.

Some agent tools can run by themselves at set times: a scheduled agent.

It can read your card base, and propose fixes, or new links.

But you approve each change by hand. An agent never changes your card base on its own.

Some cards no longer earn their place. Pruning is part of it.

To stop reviewing a card, open its menu and choose Archive card. It keeps its history.

Growing is improving too. When one part of a card keeps growing, give it its own card, and link the two.

Links need care too. When you notice two cards belong together, link them.

When a link no longer helps, delete it like a word.

Each card keeps its review history. You can delete one wrong answer, or choose Reset history to start fresh.

Want more new cards today? Click their count in the All cards menu, and press Enter.

To try Archive on your own card, choose Try it in the app.

Grow a branch here, prune one there, and your card base stays true. Next: your first week.
